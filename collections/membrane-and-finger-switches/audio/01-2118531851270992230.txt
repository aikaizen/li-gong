[calm, unhurried, contemplative teacher reading a meditation text] How do the energy channels of Daoism and Buddhism differ?. A post from November 6. [break] If Daoism (internal alchemy) circulates qi by inflating the meridians, Buddhist energy practice, which comes from Bodhidharma's Sinew-Changing and Marrow-Washing Classic, takes as its main channel the "bone membrane" (periosteum and fascia) between bone and muscle, training it until it is thick.

The reason is that meridians and nerves alike are made of microtubules, the body's biological optical fibers, and the place in the body where these microtubules are packed into the largest bundles is the membrane.

Rib purification and contemplation through the index finger: In the old Shaolin Temple they made the bone membrane thick by painfully pressing between the ribs with wooden sticks (the sinew-changing method). But if you use breathing, vocal resonance and the Former Heaven original qi, the bone membrane wrapping around the ribs swells up by itself.

On top of that, if you slowly bend the index finger (the vagus / parasympathetic switch) and contemplate while connecting your inner center (the inner side) with the painful area, old traumas (karmic imprints) embedded in the organs and the musculoskeletal system are pulled out by the roots along the cerebrospinal tracts and the autonomic nerve network.

[break] Why the membrane, of all things? How to see the form of quiescent extinction within matter.

Our body closely resembles the structure of the original nature (no-thought, no-mind, the form of quiescent extinction). If the two structures of skin and muscle, muscle and bone (the ribs), cell and cell are "no-thought" and "no-mind" respectively, then the thin membrane and gap that divides their boundary is itself the physical representation of the form of quiescent extinction.

To awaken the membrane until it is thick and, right there, to intuit the unmoving mind and the form of quiescent extinction that do not waver: this is the real secret of the Sinew-Changing Classic, proving liberation within the material structure of the body.
