[calm, unhurried, contemplative teacher reading a meditation text] '97 Yiwu wild-arbor green cake: the year of the Ding-Chou Jade Maiden. A post from November 7. [break] I'm drinking the '97 Yiwu Lü Da Shu green cake, made in 1997, a Ding-Chou year. By the logic of the stem-branches and Qimen, '97 is the year of the Ding-Chou Jade Maiden. Like a soft glow (Ding) warming the dark winter earth (Chou), it was a truly exquisite space-time for the birth of a gentle, graceful Yiwu tea, "the queen of Pu'er."

The charm of this tea is "bitter, then sweet," that is, the returning sweetness. As soon as the firm bitterness typical of wild big-tree tea brushes the mouth, the sticky sweetness of pectin and polysaccharides fermented over thirty years surges up from the root of the tongue.

[break] It is the state of sweetness within bitterness, where sweetness coexists inside the bitter.

When drinking this precious tea, take a sip from a small cup, roll it around in the mouth until it mixes well with saliva (jade liquid), and swallow slowly. Only then does the water not merely pass through as urine but become true fluids that moisten the five organs and six bowels. The sweetness the Ding-Chou Jade Maiden waited thirty years to give fills the center of the body quietly.
